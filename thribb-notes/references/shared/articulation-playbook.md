# Articulation playbook

All wording here is original. Evidence tags (like [Taylor]) point to items in source-key.md (whole items, not timestamps). No transcript text is reproduced.

Caption-derived only. Nothing here describes how the voice sounds.

## 1. Moves, with when to use them and original templates

Each move: purpose, trigger, template, failure mode.

**Concrete seed first.** Purpose: give the viewer something to hold before any abstraction. Trigger: always, in the first sentences. Template: "Here is a [object/number/puzzle]. Watch what it does." Failure: a philosophy paragraph, a history of the field, or "in this video we will cover".

**Stated goal in one plain sentence, plus a rough map.** Template: "What I want is for you to be able to ___. We will get there in three steps: ___." Evidence: many openings give the goal outright [openings].

**Spoiler-first.** Show the surprising result, then promise to earn it. Template: "This is where we will end up. It looks wrong, and it is not." Use when the payoff is striking and the route is long [Borwein, Newton].

**Audience scoping and skip-ahead.** Say who the next section is for and let people skip, while inviting the curious to stay and rediscover it. Template: "If you already know X, jump to ___. If you would rather rediscover the trick, here is the background." [quick-eigen]

**Say the viewer's objection yourself.** Purpose: trust. Template: "You could fairly say this is needlessly complicated. Here is why it is not." Pair with a visual: a small character or text bubble. [Laplace, Newton, Borwein]

**Pause invitation at a real decision point.** Not as decoration. Template: "Before I show it, take a moment: what would you predict?" Add a visible prediction beat (options on screen), then reveal. [windmills, Hanoi]

**Smallest case, then grow.** Two points, then three, then four where it gets interesting. Template: "Start with the simplest version that is not trivial." [windmills, SoME advice]

**A naive attempt that fails, then the fix.** Show the tempting wrong move, show it breaking, and use the break to motivate the next idea. Mark it wrong on screen. [hairy ball, zeta]

**Honest simplification and self-correction.** Say what you are lying about and how much. Template: "I am simplifying here, a little. The full version has a caveat about ___; you can keep it in the back of your mind." Also correct yourself in public: "I framed that earlier in a slightly misleading way." [zeta, windmills, Euler duality]

**Notation after the picture.** Name a quantity only after the viewer has seen its behavior. Say what the symbol is shorthand for. Template: "This thing we just watched has a name: ___." Also: if notation is misleading, say so. [vectors, span, Laplace]

**Motivated definitions.** Before a definition, show a situation that is awkward without it. After it, show one thing it makes easy. [span: basis defined after the viewer needs it]

**Playful names, personification and callbacks.** Give a role to each coefficient ("this one is in charge of matching the slope"), name creatures or quantities with two-word handles, then reuse them. Keep it light. [Taylor]

**Constraint accumulation.** Build a general object by adding one requirement at a time and showing that earlier requirements stay satisfied. [Taylor]

**Quantity check by a special case or quick run.** Plug in an easy number. Run a small simulation. Say "that is pretty close; take a moment to see why". [Taylor, colliding blocks]

**Reaction beats.** One short line of honest surprise or delight, placed where it happens, not stacked. Template: "That is a strange thing to be true." [zeta, Taylor]

**Scale and scope statements.** Say what the video does not cover and where to go next. Template: "I am leaving ___ for another day." [sphere-area, zeta]

**Recap by tiles.** Re-show earlier results as small labeled thumbnails when you need them again. Template: "Last time we got ___."

**Credits.** Name the person, the source, the tool. Add a visible credit card. [SoME3, Hanoi, quick-eigen last thoughts]

**Close.** One takeaway line, one thing left open, one invitation. No fanfare.

## 2. Structure patterns

- The staircase: headings follow the logic (easy case, harder case, the surprise, why). [zeta]
- The motivation sandwich: why this topic (macro), why this step (micro), pay off, look back.
- The guided exercise block: five short questions, each with a worked answer, for a proof that rewards doing. [sphere-area]
- The two-lessons close: one about people and learning, one about the mathematics. [windmills]
- The self-critique episode: show what went wrong in a prior explanation, with the failures listed and addressed one at a time. [Grover clarification]

## 3. Genres and the obligations each carries

Pick one on purpose.
- The distanced explainer: audience on the hill, state the idea from above, reveal depth in layers. Obligation: motivate every step.
- Discovery journalism: follow a real investigation; show false starts. Obligation: keep each dead end short and instructive.
- Worked example: one case, fully done, then the pattern. Obligation: choose the case that exposes the general structure.
- Demo or tool walkthrough: show the thing, then the knobs. Obligation: say what to try.
- Short: one hook under thirty seconds, one idea, one callback to the long form. Obligation: land the idea inside the clip.
- Guest or collaboration: credit them, label the style change, do not blend voices.

## 4. Word-level habits (caption statistics, all 220 items)

Per 10k words: "you" about 198, "I" about 97, "we" about 90, "so" about 63, "but" about 61, "kind of" about 16, "let's" about 13, "imagine" about 5, "notice" about 5, "pretty" about 5, beauty words about 2.7. Reading: direct address leads; "we" marks shared steps; "I" marks opinion, motive and confusion; hedges soften claims; superlatives are rare. Use these as a smell test, not a quota. Do not copy any phrase.

## 5. Anti-clone rules

- Do not reuse his catch phrases, jokes, sign-offs or running gags.
- Do not paraphrase a specific explanation line by line. Choose a different example for the same idea when you can.
- Do not name a character that looks like his recurring one; design your own or use none.
- If a user explicitly wants "in his voice", deliver the method, say you are not imitating a person, and keep the wording yours.
