Adding 1 up to n by building a rectangle

A staircase of blocks, and the picture that counts them.

Here is a staircase of blocks. It is one block wide at the top and four at the bottom.

How many blocks are there? You could count them one by one, and for four rows that works fine. But if the staircase had a hundred rows, counting would be a chore, and you would want a way that does not care about the number. So the question is really: is there a way to see the total at a glance?

A first thought: the staircase sits inside a 4 by 4 square, and a square is easy to count.

That gives 16, which is too many. The red blocks are the ones we should not have counted. Notice how they are shaped: they are also a staircase, one block short at every level. That hints that the empty space is not useless.

Reveal

Take a copy of the blue staircase and turn it upside down. In every row the blue staircase has some blocks, and the copy has exactly the missing ones. In the top row the blue has 1 and the copy has 4. In the next the blue has 2 and the copy has 3, and so on. Every row has 5 blocks in total.

So two staircases make a rectangle that is 4 blocks tall and 5 blocks wide. A rectangle is easy to count: 4 rows of 5 is 20 blocks.

4 × 5 = 20

Those 20 blocks are made of two identical staircases. Identical means they have the same count. If two equal amounts add up to 20, each is half of 20.

20 ÷ 2 = 10

So 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 = 10. You can check by counting the blocks one at a time: 1, 3, 6, 10.

Nothing special about 4

Try five rows before trusting the pattern. The same fit works, and every row now adds up to 6 blocks.

In general, with n rows, row k of the first staircase has k blocks, and row k of the copy has n + 1 − k. Their sum is k + (n + 1 − k) = n + 1 in every row, whatever k is. So the rectangle is n tall and n + 1 wide, and two copies of the sum fill it: 2 × (1 + 2 + … + n) = n × (n + 1).

Prerequisites: multiplying whole numbers, and the idea that two equal parts of a total are each half of it.
Caveat: the row-by-row argument is the whole proof, since it does not depend on n. It is shown here in words and symbols, not formally.
